\begin{abstract}
\noindent
XBoing II is a fast, colorful breakout arcade game Justin C.\ Kibell wrote in
pure Xlib between 1993 and 1996 --- the name the game shows on its title
screen, and the 2.x line recreated here. This document is a case study of
bringing it forward to a modern SDL2 codebase --- not by rewriting it in the
loose
sense, but by reimplementing it from scratch under a hard constraint:
100\% behavioral fidelity to the 1996 original, with every deviation
limited and documented. The distinguishing feature of the project is not
the port itself but the \emph{method}: the work was driven by LLM agents
operating under a principal-engineer standard, coordinated through
\emph{ethos} mission contracts (typed delegation with a worker and a
distinct evaluator), and hardened in places with \emph{z-spec} formal
methods (Z specifications model-checked with \code{probcli}). This report
documents what was built, how the agentic method worked in practice, the
two punt-labs tools that made it auditable and verifiable, and --- in
keeping with the project's ``evidence, not nostalgia'' mandate --- where
it was hard and what recurred.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Motivation}

This project had two aims. The first was to test how far agentic software
engineering has come: whether LLM agents, given the right structure, can
modernize a real, working legacy codebase to a professional standard ---
not a toy, but software people will install and play. The second was homage.
XBoing was an outstanding piece of work for its day, and it holds up: fast,
colorful, and carefully tuned, hand-built by one person over weekends on a
SPARCstation~2. Software that good deserves to keep running on current
machines, and its author deserves to see it do so.

The maintainer, who first played XBoing on an SGI Indy workstation, chose it
precisely because it is loved and because its craft rewards close study ---
the deeper the port went, the more deliberate the original's details proved
to be. When the finished port reached Justin Kibell, his reaction settled the
second aim: he built it on a fresh Linux install, played through the early
levels, and wrote that the sounds alone brought the memories --- and a tear
or two --- flooding back. He has since revived the game's homepage and joined
the project. That response is the outcome the homage was for.

\subsection{What was built}

XBoing II version 2.4 shipped on 22 November 1996 as pure Xlib --- no Motif,
no Xt --- with XPM pixmap graphics, \code{fork()}-and-\code{/dev/dsp} audio
across twelve platform-specific drivers, and compile-time paths baked in with
\code{\#define}. It then sat unmaintained for roughly two decades. (An
earlier XBoing~1 did not survive; the surviving, canonical game is the 2.x
line, which is what this port recreates. This document uses ``XBoing'' as the
short form throughout.)

The modernization produces a current SDL2 game (release 1.0.11) that
installs via Homebrew and Debian packaging and plays on contemporary Linux
and macOS. Two source trees coexist in the repository and share
\emph{zero} lines:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \filename{original/} --- Kibell's 1996 sources, preserved verbatim
        as the canonical reference. Never edited; cited line-by-line
        (\filename{original/<file>.c:<line>}) whenever behavior is
        reproduced.
  \item \filename{src/} + \filename{include/} --- the new SDL2
        implementation. The shipping game.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Reimplementation, not reinterpretation}

The precise claim is worth stating carefully, because two common framings
are both wrong. It is not a line-level port (nothing in \filename{src/} is
transliterated from \filename{original/}), and it is not a rewrite in the
sense of reimagining the game. It is a faithful \emph{reimplementation from
scratch}: new code, same game. The 1996 behavior is treated as a
specification to reproduce exactly --- level file format, scoring values,
physics constants (\code{MAX\_BALLS=5}, the 18$\times$9 grid, paddle bounce
trigonometry, \code{DIST\_BASE=30}), timing feel, the sixteen-mode state
machine. All 80 original levels load and play unchanged.

Fidelity is the default; deviations are limited and documented as
Architecture Decision Records (ADRs). Two examples: save/load v2 resumes a
ball mid-flight rather than resetting it to the paddle
(\adref{038}); and \code{PADDLE\_VELOCITY} is a known, tracked deviation
(paddle moves faster than the original) recorded in the gameplay-parity
audit. ``Faithful by default, deviations only when forced and always
documented'' is the operative rule, not a flat claim of perfection.

\section{Method: agentic software engineering}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{The standard}

The governing instruction is that every change leaves the codebase better
than it was found, with root causes proven by facts and tests rather than
asserted. Crucially, the human operator does not write the production code,
and no agent both writes a change and approves its own work. Delegation is
structured, typed, and auditable --- which is what the \emph{ethos} tooling
provides.

The two tools this report leans on, ethos and z-spec, come from Punt Labs, an
open-source effort whose thesis is to ground agentic software engineering in
formal methods and disciplined process --- rigor made cheap enough, by AI, to
apply by default. This project doubles as a test bed for that thesis; the
tools' behavior in a real modernization, including where one of them failed
(\S\ref{sec:review-layers}), is part of what the case study reports.

\subsection{Roles}

Work is organized around three roles:

\begin{description}[nosep]
  \item[Leader] (\texttt{claude}) writes the contract and reviews. It does
        not write production code solo. For non-code work (design, reports,
        reviews, investigations) it may act as the worker.
  \item[Worker] a domain specialist that executes the contract within its
        write-set --- the fixed list of files the contract permits it to change.
  \item[Evaluator] a \emph{distinct} expert persona ($\neq$ worker,
        $\neq$ leader) who judges the worker's result against the contract
        before the mission may close. The evaluator is not a generic code
        reviewer: it is a named specialist with a stake in the outcome ---
        the tester checking a fix, the original author judging whether the
        game still feels right --- and it can send the work back for another
        round (\S\ref{sec:review-layers} draws the distinction from the other
        review layers).
\end{description}

The domain specialists are modeled on the people who would actually own
each concern (\cref{tab:personas}).

\subsection{Scale}

Across the project, the tracked ledger
\filename{.punt-labs/ethos/missions.jsonl} records 113 missions ---
\MetricClosedMissions{} closed, the remainder failed or escalated rather than
quietly abandoned. By archetype they are 100 \emph{implement}, 6
\emph{design}, 5 \emph{report}, and 2 \emph{investigate}. The
worker$\to$evaluator pairings that actually ran show the specialist model in
practice: \texttt{jdc}$\to$\texttt{jck} (39),
\texttt{gjm}$\to$\texttt{jdc} (27), \texttt{jdc}$\to$\texttt{gjm} (23),
\texttt{sjl}$\to$\texttt{jdc} (9), and smaller counts. C implementation
reviewed by the tester; the tester's harnesses reviewed by the C engineer;
anything that touches game feel routed to the original author for approval.

\section{Tooling: ethos (mission contracts)}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{A mission is a contract, not a chat}

Delegated work runs as a typed contract, not an ad-hoc agent spawn. A
contract names the worker, names the evaluator (and records a hash of the
evaluator's definition, so the evaluator cannot be quietly swapped), lists
the \emph{write-set} of files the worker may change, and states
machine-checkable success criteria. A pre-commit hook rejects any edit to a
file outside the write-set, so a worker cannot widen its own scope.

The protocol runs \code{create} $\to$ \code{result} $\to$ \code{reflect}
$\to$ \code{advance} $\to$ \code{close}. The store refuses self-review,
refuses to close a mission with no submitted result, and refuses to advance
a round with no recorded reflection. None of these steps can be skipped.

\subsection{A real contract}

Consider a concrete case. The 1996 game has two hazards that crawl around
the grid: roamer blocks, which look toward a random neighbor and then shuffle
into an adjacent empty cell, and drop blocks, which fall one row at a time.
The SDL2 port had reproduced their sprites but not their motion --- the
blocks blinked in place and never moved, which made roughly fifteen levels
easier than the original. Restoring the movement is not arithmetic: it means
porting a timer-driven update that mutates the block grid while scanning it,
and doing so without letting a block move twice in one tick.

\Cref{lst:contract} is the committed contract for that fix,
\missionid{m-2026-07-15-003}. The work went to \texttt{jdc} (the C
engineer); the review went to \texttt{jck} (the game's original author), who
holds the gate on anything that changes how the game plays. The success
criteria cite the exact original source lines to match and call out the
iteration-safety hazard by name.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={The roamer/drop movement contract (excerpt, verbatim)}, label={lst:contract}]
mission_id: m-2026-07-15-003
type: implement
leader: claude
worker: jdc
evaluator:
    handle: jck
    hash: 40cbe1204f644c77e6a26757a9b855e3298be267025e806d425bfdea99054f18
write_set:
    - src/block_system.c
    - include/block_system.h
    - src/game_modes.c
success_criteria:
    - Restore ROAMER_BLK and DROP_BLK grid movement, missing from the port
      (the animation step only cycles the sprite; nothing relocates the
      block). Port original/blocks.c HandlePendingAnimations + the adjacency
      check (CheckAdjacentBlocks, 1220-1256).
    - Iteration safety (critical): the scan mutates the grid while iterating.
      A block must move AT MOST ONCE per tick. State the mechanism used.
    - Faithfulness (jck gate): on levels with roamers the blocks now wander
      to adjacent empty cells; drop blocks descend; both stay out of the
      bottom two rows and never spawn onto a ball.
    - Zero warnings under -Werror; full ctest green. Only the three
      write_set files change.
budget: { rounds: 3, reflection_after_each: true }
context: |
    Audit-2 finding, jdc-confirmed: roamer blocks only blink their eyes and
    never move; drop blocks never descend, so ~15 levels play wrong. Restore
    the original movement handler. jck gates faithfulness; a sibling mission
    adds the regression tests.
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{The reflect/advance loop does real work}

The evaluator is adversarial, and it sends work back. Mission
\missionid{m-2026-07-11-011} records the full lifecycle: the evaluator
returned \textsc{changes-requested} with three findings, forcing a second
round before the result passed. The event log is append-only:

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Mission lifecycle events (log.jsonl)}, label={lst:lifecycle}]
{"event":"create","actor":"claude"}
{"event":"reflect","details":{"converging":false,"recommendation":"continue","round":1}}
{"event":"round_advanced","details":{"from_round":1,"to_round":2}}
{"event":"result","details":{"verdict":"pass","round":2}}
{"event":"close","details":{"round":2,"status":"closed","verdict":"pass"}}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{The audit chain}

Closing a mission appends a trace line to the git-tracked ledger, and
commits carry \code{Mission:} and \code{Delegation:} trailers. The intended
reconstruction is \code{git blame} $\to$ commit $\to$ trailer $\to$
contract $\to$ prompt $\to$ full audit trail: every shipped line traces
back to the contract that authorized it and the review that approved it. On
\code{master} there are 45 \code{Mission:} trailers and 11
\code{Delegation:} trailers.

\subsection{An honest caveat}

The audit mechanism failed the first time it was relied on, and the failure
was recorded rather than hidden. When ethos was first enabled, the
commit-message hook that should have stamped the trailers automatically
stamped nothing: a session-binding bug meant commits made under an open
mission carried no trailer at all. The trailers present today were added by
hand once the bug was diagnosed and written up (PR~\#220, mission
\missionid{m-2026-09-06-009}). This is the kind of result the case study
exists to record. The tool meant to guarantee traceability was shown, in
use, not to --- and that is worth more to the next team than a clean story.

\section{Three layers of review}
\label{sec:review-layers}
% =============================================================================

Quality did not rest on the mission evaluator alone. Every change passed
through three separate layers of review, each catching a different class of
problem. Keeping them distinct is deliberate; conflating the mission
evaluator with a code reviewer would lose what each is for.

The design rests on two well-established results. First, structured review
finds defects that testing alone does not: Fagan's inspection studies
\citep{fagan1976} and Boehm and Basili's defect-reduction survey
\citep{boehm2001} both put peer review among the most cost-effective
defect-removal techniques known. Second, the reviewer must be
\emph{independent} of the author. This matters sharply for LLM agents:
Huang et al.\ \citep{huang2024} show that a model cannot reliably correct its
own reasoning without external feedback, and that debate among copies of the
same model does not supply the missing independence. That finding is the
argument for the whole arrangement below --- a distinct evaluator persona,
fixed external checklists, and third-party reviewers --- rather than asking
one agent to check its own work.

\subsection{The mission evaluator}

The first layer is the evaluator inside the mission, described above. It is a
\emph{domain expert with authority over the outcome}, not a generalist
scanning a diff. \texttt{gjm} judges whether a fix is actually tested;
\texttt{jck}, the original author, judges whether a gameplay change still
matches the 1996 game. The evaluator works against the contract's success
criteria, can force another round, and must pass before the mission closes.
It reviews intent and correctness against a specification --- before the
change is even committed.

\subsection{Local checklist reviewers}

The second layer runs after \code{make check} passes, on the committed diff,
using three fixed-checklist agents that ethos installs: \code{code-reviewer}
(general code quality and project-convention compliance),
\code{silent-failure-hunter} (swallowed errors, weak error handling, unsafe
fallbacks), and \code{invariant-completeness-reviewer} (checks that a claimed
invariant or exhaustiveness property actually holds, rather than trusting the
comment that asserts it). These are not personas and carry no domain
authority; they are mechanical passes run until each returns nothing. Where
the evaluator asks ``is this the right change, and is it proven?'', the
checklist reviewers ask ``does this diff contain a known class of mistake?''

\subsection{Remote reviewers on every pull request}

The third layer is external and runs on GitHub. All \MetricMergedPRs{}
merged pull requests went through automated reviewers --- GitHub Copilot and
Cursor's Bugbot --- under an explicit merge gate: a pull request may not
merge until the reviewers have reported on the latest commit, continuous
integration is green on that commit, and every review thread is resolved.
These reviewers see the change with fresh context and no stake in it, which
is exactly why they catch what the author and the in-project agents, both
close to the work, miss. The ledger correction in this project's own
documentation branch --- where an automated reviewer flagged that a commit
had silently reopened dozens of closed issues --- is one example of a remote
reviewer catching what every local check passed over.

Each layer is fallible on its own. Together they cover intent (the
evaluator), known defect classes (the checklists), and a fresh outside read
(the remote reviewers).

\section{Tooling: z-spec (formal methods)}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Why formal methods on a breakout game}

Most of XBoing is not a candidate for formal specification --- geometry and
rendering are better served by characterization tests. But a handful of
properties are (a) invariants the player can observe when they break, and
(b) reachable only through specific state sequences that are easy to miss
in hand-written tests. For those, the project used z-spec: a Z
specification, type-checked with \code{fuzz} and model-checked with
\code{probcli}, with each safety property proven by model-checking toward
its \emph{negation}. A reachable state matching the negation is a
counter-example that proves the defect; ``no counter-example, all states
visited'' after the fix proves the guard closes it. The technique was
applied selectively --- several ADRs explicitly record \emph{not} using it
where the logic is deterministic and stateless.

This mirrors industrial practice at a very different scale. Newcombe et
al.\ \citep{newcombe2015} report that AWS applies model checking (in their
case TLA+) precisely to the subtle, state-dependent design errors that resist
testing, and finds it both feasible and cost-effective for mainstream
engineering. A 1996 breakout game is not a distributed data store, but the
class of bug is the same: a bad state reachable only through a particular
sequence of events, invisible to example-based tests.

\subsection{The ammo belt}

The player's remaining bullets appear three times over: the true count the
gun fires from, the row of icons drawn on the heads-up display, and the
tally paid out on the end-of-level bonus screen. In the original these three
always agree. The port had let them drift: the display code hid the icons
when ammo was unlimited and clamped the count at a maximum, while the bonus
screen still read the raw figure. The result was visible --- pick up a
max-ammo block, finish the level, and the belt showed zero while the bonus
paid out twenty-one.

The specification \filename{docs/bullet\_ammo.tex} models all three
quantities and states the invariant that the display must equal the true
count. Model-checking the code as written, the property that the display and
the bonus agree failed in 441 of 486 reachable states --- the whole region
where the drift could occur. With the fix (draw the true count, with no
special case for unlimited ammo and no clamp), that region collapses: the
invariant holds in every remaining reachable state. The change is
recorded in \adref{063} and pinned in the code by a regression test that
fails on the old behavior and passes on the new. The formal model, the
regression test, and the fix are three statements of one property.

\subsection{The screen state machine}

The specification \filename{docs/specs/2026-07-04-screen-state-machine.tex}
models the flat mode machine and its shared resources, with three safety
invariants (stated deliberately as observer schemas so that violating
transitions stay reachable rather than being silently disabled):

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Screen-machine safety invariants (Z)}, label={lst:zschema}]
SafeGame:      mode = mGame => blocksLoaded = true
SafeHighscore: (mode = mHighscore and boardChoice = bGlobal
                 and globalHasData = false) => personalHasData = false
SafeAttract:   (mode in attractModes and mode /= mHighscore)
                 => gameActive = false
\end{lstlisting}

Each invariant named a real defect. Model-checking found a reachable state
that violated it; after the fix, an exhaustive pass found none.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{The empty Hall of Fame.} The attract loop cycles through a
        high-score screen. On an install with no shared global score file
        (the common case on a personal machine), that screen came up blank
        instead of falling back to the player's own scores.
        \code{SafeHighscore} required the fallback; the fix (\adref{054})
        made it hold across 138 states.
  \item \textbf{The leaked game flag.} A single flag marks whether a game is
        in progress, and it gates score submission. After a game ended, the
        flag was not always cleared before the attract loop resumed, so an
        idle demo screen could still look like live play. \code{SafeAttract}
        forbade that; a seven-step counter-example showed exactly how the
        flag escaped, and the fix (\adref{055}) closed it across 90 states.
  \item \textbf{The empty board.} Pressing Space starts a game. If the level
        file failed to load, the game started anyway --- straight into the
        end-of-level bonus screen over an empty grid, because ``no blocks
        left'' and ``no blocks loaded'' looked the same. \code{SafeGame}
        required blocks to be loaded before play begins; the fix (\adref{056})
        made it hold across 76 states.
\end{itemize}

Each fix is both a re-proven invariant and a regression test that fails on
the old code and passes on the new.

\subsection{A caveat on the artifacts}

One committed artifact,
\filename{docs/specs/2026-07-04-screen-state-machine.report.json}, is a raw
\code{zspec test} run at a small set size that deadlocks --- it is a
tooling snapshot, distinct from the goal-directed proofs above. The
authoritative results are the ``all states visited'' outcomes in the
specification's verification section and the ADRs, not that JSON.

\section{Testing and fidelity}
% =============================================================================

Because the goal was to preserve behavior, testing led the work rather than
following it. This is the discipline Feathers \citep{feathers2004} calls
characterization testing: before changing legacy code, write tests that
capture what it currently does, then hold that behavior fixed as the code
moves. The test strategy is a five-layer pyramid: (1) \code{cmocka} unit
tests of pure logic; (2) integration tests running the game loop headless
with the SDL dummy drivers; (3) replay/golden tests of full input sequences;
(4) visual-fidelity tests that capture screenshots and compare them against
the 1996 binary, including an LLM-judged comparison; and (5) parser fuzzing.
The savegame-v2 fixture mechanism (\adref{038}) makes the deeper layers
practical: a test can seed an arbitrary game state --- level 25, a specific
block mid-cooldown, a ball in flight --- directly through the save/restore
path, instead of replaying thousands of input frames to reach it.

Fidelity was verified subsystem by subsystem through six parity audits ---
audio volume, editor, gameplay logic, animation cadence, key commands, and
sound effects --- each comparing the modern build against
\filename{original/} and filing discrepancies as beads. Not every audit
finding was a real bug: at least one (bonus-screen tally ``too slow'') was
a false positive, and the pacing turned out to be an intentional
readability trade-off. Recording the false positives is part of the
evidence.

\section{What was hard, and what recurred}
% =============================================================================

In keeping with the project's mandate --- evidence, not nostalgia --- the
honest retrospective matters as much as the wins.

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Documentation drift.} The retrospective docs froze at the
        February ``100\% complete'' milestone and did not track the large
        body of work that landed after it; this very report is the
        correction. The lesson: a single machine-readable metrics source
        (\filename{docs/metrics.json}) beats prose numbers that silently
        rot.
  \item \textbf{The traceability tool's own bug.} The trailer-stamping
        hook was broken the first time ethos was enabled; relying on the
        audit mechanism in earnest is what surfaced it.
  \item \textbf{Config contradictions.} Three project files independently
        encoded the same facts and drifted apart, requiring a
        reconciliation pass (PR \#222). Single-source-of-truth is a
        recurring failure mode, not a one-off.
  \item \textbf{Faithful-looking bugs that are not bugs.} Several
        ``deviations'' (the ammo morph-block scoring zero, the
        $\approx$22.5s auto-launch delay, the bonus tally pacing) were
        verified \emph{faithful} to 1996 and deliberately kept. Fidelity
        work is as much about resisting spurious fixes as making real ones.
\end{itemize}
